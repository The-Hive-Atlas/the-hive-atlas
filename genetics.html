Honey bee genetic diversity

Mitochondrial DNA results for honey bee colonies: which evolutionary lineage each colony belongs to, which haplotypes are common, and how varied each area’s bees are.

A snapshot, not a census. Each state or area is represented by the colonies that were sampled and genotyped, so these numbers describe the colonies tested, not every colony there. Areas with fewer than 10 colonies are outlined with a dashed line.

Neutral on naming. The Hive Atlas team remains neutral on how other investigators name lineages, haplotypes, and subspecies. Names are shown exactly as reported in the original studies; showing them here does not endorse any naming or classification scheme, and the team takes no side where studies disagree.

What the map shows

Results from mitochondrial DNA testing of honey bee colonies in 47 countries, gathered in the Genetic Database 2026: the 2019 U.S. survey of 1,063 colonies plus published studies from Europe, Africa, the Middle East, and the Americas. For each colony, the data record its evolutionary lineage and its haplotype, the specific version of the mitochondrial DNA it carries.

Pick a country with the Country menu. The United States is shown with state shapes. Other countries are shown as a country outline with one dot per area named in the source data (a region, province, or state), placed at that area’s reference point; bigger dots mean more colonies.

Mitochondrial DNA is passed down from the queen, so these results trace the maternal line of each colony. They do not describe the drones, and they do not measure how much of a colony’s genome comes from each ancestry.

Only summarized counts by country, area, and year are published. Individual colonies, sample numbers, and apiary locations are not.

The measures

  • Lineage percents (C, M, A): the share of colonies with a recorded lineage in the area assigned to each lineage.
  • Haplotype diversity: the chance that two colonies picked at random from the area carry different haplotypes. It runs from 0 (all the same) to 1 (all different). It is calculated from the haplotype counts (the database’s Haplotype column, for every country) with the unbiased estimator of Nei, which corrects for small samples, so it can be pooled for climate regions and whole countries. Colonies with no recorded haplotype are left out.
  • Colonies genotyped: how many colonies stand behind each number.

The lineages

  • C, eastern European. This includes the most widely managed breeds. In the haplotype list, C1 is ligustica (Italian) bees, C2 is carnica, and C3 is Buckfast.
  • M, western and northern European, the lineage of the dark bee.
  • A, African. Dominant in Africa and much of the Middle East in this data set; in the United States found most often in Puerto Rico and the Gulf states.

How to use it

  • Continent narrows the Country menu to one continent and opens a map of that continent, with each country colored by the colonies sampled there. Russia is listed under both Europe and Asia, because its samples come from both. Pick “All continents” to go back to the full list.
  • Country picks the country shown on the map, in the panel, and in the table. Worldwide pools every country: the world map colors each country by its own value, and the table lists one row per country. Click a country, then “Open the … map” to see its areas.
  • Area (United States only) shows states, or the nine NOAA climate regions plus Alaska and Hawaii. Regions pool several states, so they are steadier when state counts are small.
  • Color the map by picks the measure.
  • Reference, under the map, lists the published studies (with DOI where recorded) behind the selected country’s data, with the number of colonies each one contributed. For a continent or the worldwide view, open the list to see every study in that view.
  • Subspecies lists the subspecies recorded in the selected country, as named by the source studies, most common first, with the number of colonies for each. Pick one to color the map by the share of colonies (with a recorded subspecies) assigned to it; pick “All subspecies” or another measure to go back.
  • Haplotype lists the haplotypes found in the selected country, most common first, with the number of colonies carrying each. Pick one to color the map by the share of colonies carrying it; pick “All haplotypes” or another measure to go back.
  • Click a state, region, or dot to see its lineage makeup and most common haplotypes, and how it compares with the whole country.
  • The table lists every state or area with colonies genotyped. Click a heading to sort, or a row to open it.

Small samples

Most U.S. states have 20 to 25 genotyped colonies, and many areas elsewhere have fewer. With so few colonies, a single colony can shift a percent by several points. Areas with fewer than 10 colonies are outlined with a dashed line on the map and marked in the table, and they are left out of the ranks.

Limits

  • Colonies were sampled in different years by different studies (from 2001 to 2024), and some records have no year. The map pools all years, so it does not show change over time.
  • Studies differ in how finely they name haplotypes, so diversity is best compared within a country rather than between countries.
  • North Carolina, New Hampshire, Ohio, Rhode Island, and Wyoming have no genotyped colonies. They appear in gray.
  • The data are from maternal lines only. A colony with a C-lineage queen can still have drones and workers with other ancestry.
  • Colonies were not drawn at random from every state, so the numbers should not be read as the exact makeup of each state’s bees.
  • Guam, Puerto Rico, and U.S. colonies with no recorded state are in the table and the U.S. totals but not on the map. Outside the U.S., colonies with no recorded location are in the table and the country totals but not on the map.
