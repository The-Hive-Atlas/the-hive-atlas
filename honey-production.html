What the map shows

How much honey U.S. beekeepers produced each year, state by state, how much each colony made, what beekeepers were paid for it, and how much honey they still held at the end of the year. The numbers come from the annual Honey report of the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS), which covers every year since 1987.

NASS surveys a sample of operations with five or more colonies that qualify as farms, meaning they sold, or would normally sell, at least $1,000 of agricultural products a year. Each year’s report comes out in March and covers the previous year’s honey crop.

How to use it

  • Map style switches between hexagons, where every state is the same size and easy to click, and real state shapes. The data is identical in both.
  • Color the map by picks the measure. Honey per colony comes first because it compares fairly across states of any size and is the number most beekeepers can hold up against their own hives.
  • Time period shows a single year or an average over several years. Use the arrows to step through years one at a time.
  • Prices and values shows dollar amounts either adjusted for inflation to 2025 dollars (the default) or as NASS reported them. See Adjusting for inflation.
  • Click a state to see its numbers, its rank, how it compares with the U.S., and its trend since 1987. Click a bar in the trend chart to jump the map to that year.
  • The table lists every published state for the period. Click a heading to sort, or a row to open that state.
  • Download the table you are looking at, a state’s full history from its panel, or the complete dataset. See Downloads.

Adjusting for inflation

A dollar in 1987 bought about 2.8 times what it buys today, so prices as reported make honey look far more valuable now than it was. By default, the map shows prices and values in 2025 dollars, so every year can be compared fairly.

How it’s calculated. Each year’s dollars are multiplied by the 2025 Consumer Price Index divided by that year’s index, using the yearly average of the CPI for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. For example, the U.S. price in 1987 was $0.50 a pound as reported, which is $1.43 in 2025 dollars (0.503 × 321.9 ÷ 113.6).

The CPI measures what households pay for everything they buy, not what it costs to keep bees, so the adjusted price shows honey’s buying power, not beekeepers’ profit. Switch to As reported to see NASS’s figures unchanged. The downloads include both.

Multi-year averages

Honey crops swing with weather, forage, and colony health, so a single year can be misleading, especially for smaller states. The multi-year view averages several years to show how each state typically does.

  • Each value is the average of the yearly values in the window. For honey per colony, that is the typical yield of a year in that state.
  • The windows end in 2023, the last year most states were published separately, so every state is compared over the same years. One more option, each state’s full record, uses every year a state was published, including 2024 and later for the 20 states still published.
  • A state needs at least 80% of the years in a window to get a value.

Why some states are in Other States

NASS combines states it doesn’t publish separately into a single Other States group. From 1987 to 1990 it published 49 states individually. Through 2023 it published 40 to 45, and starting with the 2024 crop it publishes 20. NASS’s report says only that Other States includes the states not shown in its table.

The 20 states still published are California, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Iowa, Louisiana, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Montana, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, South Dakota, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. They produce most of the country’s honey.

On the map, a state in Other States is gray. Clicking it shows the Other States numbers, which describe the group as a whole and not that state alone.

Migratory beekeeping

Many commercial colonies travel during the year, so the same colony can make honey in more than one state. NASS counts such a colony in every state where it produced honey. Each state’s honey per colony is therefore the honey made in that state divided by the colonies that made honey there.

This matters most in the Northern Plains. North Dakota, South Dakota, and Montana host large numbers of commercial colonies during their summer honey flow, which is why North Dakota alone produces about a quarter of U.S. honey, and many of those colonies spend winter and almond season in other states. A state’s numbers describe its honey crop, not necessarily how its own beekeepers fared.

U.S. totals

  • Honey per colony. Because a colony that made honey in two states is counted in both, the U.S. colony total counts some colonies twice. NASS notes this makes the U.S. honey per colony read somewhat low. U.S. honey produced is not affected.
  • Values. NASS calculates the U.S. value from U.S. production and the U.S. average price, so it won’t exactly equal the sum of the states. Across all years the gap is under 2.5%.
  • Comparing with the U.S. For honey per colony and price, a state is compared with the U.S. average. For totals, a state is shown as a share of the U.S.

Colors and ranks

Each measure has five color ranges, set from every state’s values across all years. The ranges stay the same as you change years, so a color means the same thing in 1990 and in 2025. Inflation-adjusted and as-reported dollars each have their own ranges.

Ranks compare a state with the other states published for the same period, and 1 is the highest value. Since only 20 states are published from 2024 on, ranks from those years are out of 20 instead of about 40.

Gray means the state is counted in Other States. Striped means NASS did not publish that number, or, for a multi-year average, that the state was published for too few of the years.

Values are as NASS published them, except that prices before 2018, which NASS reported in cents per pound, are converted to dollars. Please cite USDA NASS as the source and The Hive Atlas as the compiler.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Commercial and sideline operations only. The survey covers operations with five or more colonies that qualify as farms. Backyard beekeepers are not included.
  • Survey estimates. These are statistical estimates from a sample of operations. NASS revises the previous year’s prices, values, and stocks when it publishes a new report, so the latest year may change.
  • Rounded values. NASS rounds state colonies to the nearest thousand and state honey per colony to the nearest pound.
  • Colonies move. A state’s numbers describe the honey made there, not the colonies owned by its beekeepers. See Migratory beekeeping.
  • Not comparable with Colony Losses. Honey-producing colonies count colonies honey was taken from during the year; the Colony Losses survey counts colonies on specific dates.
