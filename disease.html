What the map shows

Results from adult honey bee samples tested by the Bee Disease Diagnostic Laboratory (BDDL) at the USDA Agricultural Research Service Bee Research Laboratory in Beltsville, Maryland. The lab has diagnosed honey bee samples from across the country, and from beekeepers and inspectors in other countries, free of charge for decades. This map covers about 85,000 samples received from 1984 to September 2026: about 46,000 samples of adult bees and about 39,000 samples of brood (comb or larvae), from 38 countries. Nearly all (about 97%) come from the United States; about 2,200 come from Canada, and a few to several dozen from each of 36 other countries.

Adult bee samples are tested for three parasites:

  • Varroa mites (Varroa destructor), the external parasite most closely tied to colony losses, which also spreads viruses.
  • Nosema (Nosema apis and Nosema ceranae, also called Vairimorpha), a microsporidian gut parasite of adult bees.
  • Tracheal mites (Acarapis woodi), which live in the breathing tubes of adult bees.

Brood samples are tested for two bacterial diseases of developing bees:

  • American foulbrood (AFB), caused by Paenibacillus larvae. Its spores survive for decades, it is highly contagious, and many states require infected colonies to be burned.
  • European foulbrood (EFB), caused by Melissococcus plutonius. It is usually less severe than AFB but has become much more common in recent years.

Adult and brood samples are separate submissions, so the two sets of results come from different samples and are counted separately.

Where samples come from, and why it matters

State apiary inspectors and beekeepers send samples to the lab when a colony shows signs of disease, weakness, or decline. That makes this a diagnostic record, not a random survey of U.S. colonies, and it shapes how the numbers should be read:

  • Rates run high. A state where 60% of samples carried Nosema doesn’t have Nosema in 60% of its colonies. It means 60% of the samples sent in from struggling colonies tested positive. Studies that compared BDDL results with the USDA APHIS National Honey Bee Disease Survey, which samples apparently healthy apiaries, found much higher mite and spore levels in BDDL samples.
  • Who sends samples changes the numbers. Some states have inspection programs that send many samples, and others send few or none. A change in a state’s inspection program can change its numbers as much as a change in bee health. Ohio, New York, and South Dakota together sent more than 60% of all U.S. adult samples, and Ohio alone sent more than a quarter of all U.S. brood samples.
  • Sample counts change a lot over time. The lab received about 2,200 to 3,200 adult samples a year from 2008 to 2012, but only about 130 to 450 a year from 1986 to 1990 and in the early 2000s, and about 230 to 390 a year since 2021. Brood samples ranged from about 240 to 3,000 a year.
  • Samples from other countries are few. Outside the United States and Canada, most countries sent fewer than 30 samples in all, so their numbers describe a handful of colonies, not the country.

The map is most useful for seeing what is turning up in problem colonies, where, in which months, and how that has shifted over the years.

How to use it

  • Continent narrows the Country menu to one continent and opens a map of that continent, with each country colored by its samples. Pick “All continents” to go back to the full list, which also has a Worldwide view.
  • Country picks the United States (state shapes), another country (its outline, with one dot per province or area; Canadian samples are recorded by province, other countries as a whole), all countries in a continent, or the whole world. When you open a view, the years are set to the years it has samples if the current years have none.
  • Area (United States only) shows states, or the nine NOAA climate regions plus Alaska and Hawaii. Regions pool many states, so they are steadier when state counts are small.
  • Color the map by picks the disease and the measure: percent of samples positive, average load, or number of samples. Adult bee diseases and foulbrood are grouped separately in the menu.
  • Years shows one year or a range of years. A range pools every sample in those years, which steadies small states.
  • Months limits the view to one season. Mite and spore levels change a lot through the year, so comparing the same season across years or states is fairer.
  • Click a state, region, province, or country to see its numbers, how it compares with the total for the view (the U.S., the country, the continent, or the world), its pattern by month, and its trend by year. In a continent or worldwide view, the side panel has a button to open that country’s own map. Click a bar in the trend chart to jump to that year.
  • Reference, under the map, lists the sources cited in the lab workbook for the selected country, continent, or worldwide view, with DOI links and the number of samples behind each one. It follows the years and months you pick. Samples the workbook marks as unpublished, or gives no reference for, are listed too.
  • The table lists every state, province, or country with samples for the period. Click a heading to sort, or a row to open that state.

Each sample is one colony. A single submission can include samples from several colonies, occasionally hundreds, and each colony’s sample is counted separately. These follow the definitions in the lab’s published studies.

Lab methods

Adult bee samples are about 100 to 300 adult worker bees, collected from one colony and shipped in alcohol.

  • Varroa: bees are washed in alcohol with steady pressure for about 40 seconds over a #8 mesh screen, which separates the mites. Mites are counted, and the number of bees is calculated from their wet weight (about 0.16 grams per bee).
  • Nosema: the abdomens of 30 bees are crushed in 30 mL of water, and spores are counted with a hemocytometer under a microscope at 400× magnification.
  • Tracheal mites: 16 bees are dissected. A thin disk is cut from each thorax, cleared in lactic acid, and the breathing tubes are examined under a dissecting microscope.

Brood samples are usually a section of brood comb about 2 by 2 inches, sometimes a whole frame, or a “brood smear” that an inspector collects in the field by swabbing diseased cells.

  • Antibiotic resistance of American foulbrood: Paenibacillus larvae from AFB-positive samples is grown on agar with a paper disc of tetracycline (oxytetracycline) or tylosin, the two antibiotics used against AFB in the United States. The width of the clear zone around the disc shows how well the antibiotic stops the bacteria. Methods are in Alburaki et al. (2024).
  • American and European foulbrood: samples are first inspected visually. Material from diseased cells is then smeared on a slide, dried, stained with carbol fuchsin, and examined under a microscope at 1,000× magnification. The two diseases are told apart by the shape of the bacteria and their spores.

Full methods are in Abban et al. (2024), Alburaki et al. (2024), and Alburaki et al. (2025), listed under Sources.

The climate regions are the nine U.S. climate regions defined by NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information (Karl and Koss, 1984), the same ones used in the lab’s published studies and on the Colony Monitoring page. Alaska and Hawaii are shown on their own. The District of Columbia is in the Northeast. Puerto Rico isn’t in a region.

Seasonal expectation

Mite loads, spore counts, and foulbrood all rise and fall through the year, and states send samples at different times. A state that mostly sent winter samples can look worse than one that mostly sent summer samples, even if both are typical for their season. Shade by: vs. seasonal expectation takes that into account.

  • Expected value: for every sample, the national result for the same calendar month, taken from the lab’s own samples over a five-year moving window (the sample’s year and the two years on either side). Adding these up gives what an area would show if each of its samples were typical for its month. The moving window follows long-term changes, such as the rise of European foulbrood since about 2010. If fewer than 30 samples nationally fall in a month’s window, the whole window is used instead.
  • Compared with what was observed: for percent positive, the difference in percentage points (“12.9 points above expected”).
  • Within the expected range: a difference is only colored when it is larger than chance alone would easily produce for that many samples (about two standard errors). Otherwise the area is shown in the neutral color. This keeps a state with three samples from looking dramatic.
  • Much higher or lower: 15 or more percentage points from the expectation, or a load at least twice (or under half) the expectation.

The seasonal expectation is available for the United States only, because it is built from U.S. samples. In other views the map is shaded by rate.

The comparison is with the lab’s own national samples, which come from colonies with problems, so “higher than expected” means higher than other diagnostic samples from the same time of year, not higher than the average colony. Samples with no month recorded are compared with the whole window. The By month chart in each panel shows the same comparison month by month: the dark tick on each bar is the U.S. value for that month.

Small samples

Many states send only a handful of samples in a year. Every value is shown, but those based on fewer than 10 samples are marked: a dashed outline on the map, a Small sample flag in the state panel, and a ! in the table. With one or two samples, a state can read 0% or 100%. Use a range of years or climate regions for a steadier picture.

Ranks only include states with 10 or more samples tested, and the color ranges are set from state-years with at least 10 samples.

Data cleaning

The data come from the cleaned lab workbook, which covers every country. Each row is one sample, coded 0 (not found) or 1 (found) for each test. A few cells (14 Varroa) hold a value of 2 to 4; with one sample per row they are counted as positive. 93 U.S. samples with no usable state were left out. Varroa results before 1990 are treated as not tested, as described above.

Zip codes, sample numbers, and submitter details are never published; the most detailed level on this site is the state.

Colors and ranks

Each measure has five color ranges, set from every state’s values across all years (or every region’s, in the region view). The ranges stay the same as you change years or seasons, so a color means the same thing in 1995 and in 2025.

Gray means no samples came from that state in the period. Striped means samples came in but weren’t tested for that measure. Ranks compare a state with the other states that had 10 or more samples tested in the same period, and 1 is the highest value.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Not a prevalence survey. Rates describe samples from colonies with problems. See Where samples come from.
  • Location is where the sample was sent from. Samples are mapped to the state on the submission. Colonies that move for pollination may have picked up parasites elsewhere.
  • Methods changed over 40 years. Lab staff, equipment, and sample volumes changed over time, so small shifts between decades should be read with care.
  • 2026 is a partial year. Samples run through September 2026, so 2026 totals can’t be compared directly with full years.
