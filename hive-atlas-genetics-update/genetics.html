What the map shows

Results from mitochondrial DNA testing of honey bee colonies sampled across the United States in 2019. For each colony, the data record its evolutionary lineage and its haplotype, the specific version of the mitochondrial DNA it carries.

Mitochondrial DNA is passed down from the queen, so these results trace the maternal line of each colony. They do not describe the drones, and they do not measure how much of a colony’s genome comes from each ancestry.

Only summarized counts by state are published. Individual colonies, sample numbers, and apiary locations are not.

The measures

  • Lineage percents (M or A, C, M, A): the share of genotyped colonies in the area assigned to each lineage. “M or A” adds the M and A lineages together, which shows where bees with non-C maternal ancestry turn up.
  • Haplotype diversity: the chance that two colonies picked at random from the area carry different haplotypes. It runs from 0 (all the same) to 1 (all different). It is calculated from the counts with the unbiased estimator of Nei, which corrects for small samples, so it can be pooled for climate regions and the whole country.
  • Novel haplotypes: the share of colonies carrying a haplotype flagged as novel in the source data, meaning one that did not match a previously published sequence.
  • Colonies genotyped: how many colonies stand behind each number.

The lineages

  • C, eastern European. This includes the most widely managed breeds. In the haplotype list, C1 is ligustica (Italian) bees, C2 is carnica, and C3 is Buckfast.
  • M, western and northern European, the lineage of the dark bee.
  • A, African, in this data set found most often in Puerto Rico and the Gulf states (Louisiana, Florida, and Texas), with a few colonies elsewhere.
  • O, Middle Eastern. No colonies in this data set were assigned to it.

How to use it

  • Area shows states, or the nine NOAA climate regions plus Alaska and Hawaii. Regions pool several states, so they are steadier when state counts are small.
  • Color the map by picks the measure.
  • Click a state or region to see its lineage makeup and most common haplotypes, and how it compares with the U.S.
  • The table lists every state with colonies genotyped. Click a heading to sort, or a row to open that state.

Small samples

Each state has between 6 and 37 genotyped colonies, usually 20 to 25. With so few colonies, a single colony can shift a state’s percent by several points. States with fewer than 10 colonies are outlined on the map and marked in the table, and they are left out of the ranks.

Limits

  • One survey year (2019). The map cannot show change over time yet.
  • North Carolina, New Hampshire, Ohio, Rhode Island, and Wyoming have no genotyped colonies. They appear in gray.
  • The data are from maternal lines only. A colony with a C-lineage queen can still have drones and workers with other ancestry.
  • Colonies were not drawn at random from every state, so the numbers should not be read as the exact makeup of each state’s bees.
  • Guam and Puerto Rico are in the table and the U.S. totals but not on the map.
