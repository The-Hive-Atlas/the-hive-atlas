What the map shows

How many honey bee colonies U.S. beekeepers lost each quarter, state by state, and which stressors affected their colonies. The numbers come from the quarterly Colony Loss Survey run by the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS), which has surveyed beekeeping operations since January 2015. To be in the survey, an operation needs five or more colonies and has to qualify as a farm, meaning it sold, or would normally sell, at least $1,000 of agricultural products a year.

Each quarter covers three months: January–March, April–June, July–September, and October–December. Losses follow the seasons, so winter quarters usually run higher than summer ones. Compare a quarter with the same quarter in other years rather than with the quarter before it.

How to use it

  • Map style switches between hexagons, where every state is the same size and easy to click, and real state shapes. The data is identical in both.
  • Color the map by picks the measure. Losses and renovations come first, then the six stressors.
  • Time period shows a single quarter, a full year, or the whole survey. Use the arrows to step through periods one at a time.
  • Go to a state opens a state by name or two-letter code.
  • Click a state to see its numbers, its rank, how it compares with the U.S., its stressors, and its trend. Click a bar in the trend chart to jump the map to that period.
  • The table lists every published state for the period. Click a heading to sort, or a row to open that state.

The year view

NASS publishes quarterly numbers only, so the year view combines the quarters:

  • Percents (lost, renovated, and stressors) are the average of the year’s quarters: (Q1 + Q2 + Q3 + Q4) ÷ 4.
  • Colonies lost is the year’s total: Q1 + Q2 + Q3 + Q4.
  • Colonies is the count on January 1.

For example, U.S. losses in 2024 were 15%, 9%, 10%, and 14%, so the average quarterly loss is 12.0%.

This is not an annual loss rate, and the page labels it “average quarterly loss” for that reason. Beekeepers replace and split colonies all year, and each quarter’s percent uses its own count of colonies, so there is no single exact way to turn quarterly rates into a yearly one. Other common methods give much higher numbers for the same 2024 data:

MethodFormulaU.S. 2024
Average quarterly loss (used here)Average of the four quarterly percents12.0%
Compounded loss1 − (1 − Q1)(1 − Q2)(1 − Q3)(1 − Q4), the chance a colony doesn’t survive all four quarters if none were replaced40.1%
Total lost ÷ January 1 colonies1,353,350 ÷ 2,669,35050.7%

A state gets a yearly value only when NASS published it for every quarter of that year. 2019 averages three quarters, because no data was published for April–June 2019, and the latest year averages only the quarters released so far. Both are labeled in the period menu.

The whole-survey view

A single quarter is noisy, especially for smaller states, where losses can swing 10 points from one quarter to the next. The whole-survey view averages many quarters to show how each state typically fares.

How it’s calculated. Losses are much higher in some seasons than others, so a plain average would favor states with missing winter quarters. Instead, each season is averaged on its own (every January–March quarter, every April–June quarter, and so on), and the result is the average of those four season values. A state needs at least 3 published quarters in every season to get a value. Percents are averages of quarterly percents, and colony counts are averages per quarter.

Two windows. States were not all published for the same years, which would mix good and bad years unevenly. So there are two options:

  • Common window (the default) uses the same quarters for every state: from the first survey quarter through the last quarter when most states were still published separately (mid-2024). Use this one to compare and rank states.
  • Each state’s full record uses every quarter a state was published. It includes more recent data for the 20 states still published, but those states then cover different years from the rest.

Like the year view, this is an average quarterly loss, not an annual loss rate.

By season. Every state panel shows the typical value for each quarter of the year over the common window, so you can see when a state’s losses tend to happen as well as how large they are.

Trends and precision. The quarterly chart in each panel shows whether a state has done better or worse over time. We don’t run a formal trend test: roughly ten years of noisy survey estimates can rarely show a real change with confidence. NASS doesn’t publish a margin of error for loss rates. Each state panel shows how many operations the estimate rests on (see Sample sizes); even with that, treat states a point or two apart in a whole-survey ranking as effectively tied.

Why so many states are in Other States

NASS combines states with too few reporting operations into a single Other States group. From 2015 through April–June 2024, it published 44 or 45 states individually. Starting with July–September 2024, it publishes 20.

NASS’s data quality report for the August 2025 release says estimates are published for 20 states and for Other States, “which includes thirty States with low levels of inventory.” We have not found a published explanation for the change.

The 20 states still published hold most of the country’s colonies, about 89% in April–June 2025. They are California, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Iowa, Louisiana, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Montana, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, South Dakota, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin.

On the map, a state in Other States is gray. Clicking it shows the Other States numbers, which describe the group as a whole and not that state alone.

Migratory beekeeping

Many commercial colonies travel across the country during the year, to pollinate almonds in California, to overwinter in the South, and back to summer honey ranges in the Northern Plains. The survey handles this in a specific way, and it changes what a state’s numbers mean.

Colonies count in every state they visit. Operations report their colonies separately for each state where they were during the quarter, so a colony moved from North Dakota to California counts in both states’ peak colonies that quarter. That’s why state peaks add up to more than the national total, and why NASS doesn’t publish a national peak.

Each lost colony counts once, in the state where it was. For each state, the survey asks how many of the colonies located there were lost during the quarter. A colony from North Dakota that dies in California in February is a California loss. Lost colonies in every state add up exactly to the U.S. total, so losses are not double-counted.

So a state’s loss rate describes colonies while they were in that state, not how that state’s beekeepers fared over a year. North Dakota shows this clearly. Averages for the first day of each quarter, 2015 to mid-2024:

North Dakota has about seven times as many colonies in summer as in winter. Most of its colonies spend winter and almond season in other states, so losses during the riskiest part of the year are counted there, and North Dakota’s low overall rate partly reflects that. California’s winter losses, in turn, include colonies trucked in from across the country.

When comparing states, keep in mind that summer honey states and winter destination states see different parts of the same colonies’ year. The By season breakdown in each state panel shows when a state’s losses happen.

Sample sizes

NASS surveys a sample of operations in each state, not every beekeeper. Its yearly data quality reports, available from 2019 on, give the number of operations sampled in each state every quarter and the share that responded. Each state panel shows these as “Based on X operations sampled; about Y responded,” and the table has a Sampled column. For a year or the whole survey, the typical (median) quarter is shown.

Small samples are flagged. When fewer than 20 operations responded, the panel shows a Small sample flag and the table marks the state. With so few operations, one or two large ones can drive the whole state’s loss rate, and the number can swing a lot from quarter to quarter. NASS samples every large or multi-state operation and a random selection of smaller ones, so large operations are always included.

What isn’t shown. NASS doesn’t report how colonies are spread across operations, so the page can’t show whether one operation holds most of a state’s colonies. The quality reports also include a coefficient of variation, but it measures the precision of the colony counts, not the loss rates, so it isn’t shown here. Sample sizes before 2019 were not published.

Matching quarters. NASS labels each quality table by the survey date (January 1, April 1, July 1, October 1). Each is matched to the quarter that begins on that date. NASS notes the July 2019 survey wasn’t conducted, while the loss data is missing April–June 2019 instead, so sample sizes for that part of 2019 may be shifted by one quarter. Sample sizes change little from quarter to quarter, so this has almost no effect.

Corrections

Values are shown as NASS published them, with one exception. Every published percent is checked against NASS’s own colony counts, and in two cases they contradict each other:

State and quarterNASS countsPublished percent lostShown here
Wyoming, Jul–Sep 2023860 lost of 23,000 peak coloniesunder 0.5%3.7%†
Wyoming, Oct–Dec 20231,700 lost of 18,500 peak coloniesunder 0.5%9.2%†

The counts appear to be right: together with every other state they add up exactly to NASS’s U.S. total, and they fit Wyoming’s losses in other years. So the published percents appear to be the error, and the page shows the percent calculated from the counts, marked with †. These corrected values are also used in the year and whole-survey averages. The downloadable data keeps NASS’s values as published. If NASS corrects its table, the correction here will be removed.

Colors and ranks

Each measure has five color ranges, set from every state’s values across all periods. The ranges stay the same as you change periods, so a color means the same thing in 2016 and in 2025. The quarter and year views each have their own ranges.

When a state is compared with the U.S., keep the denominators in mind: state percents use each state’s peak colony count, which is larger than its start count, while the U.S. uses the start count. State percents therefore tend to read a little lower than the national figure. Comparing states with each other is like for like.

Ranks compare a state with the other states published for the same period, and 1 is the highest value. Since only 20 states are published from July–September 2024 on, ranks from those periods are out of 20 instead of about 45.

Gray means the state is counted in Other States. Striped means NASS did not publish that number, for example in April–June 2019, or for a year when the state was not published every quarter.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Commercial and sideline operations only. The survey covers operations with five or more colonies that qualify as farms. Backyard beekeepers are not included.
  • Colonies move. Many commercial colonies travel between states for pollination, so a state’s numbers describe the colonies there that quarter, not the colonies owned by its beekeepers. See Migratory beekeeping.
  • Survey estimates. These are statistical estimates from a sample of operations, and NASS revises the previous year’s numbers when it publishes a new report. Smaller states rest on fewer sampled operations; in January 2024, for example, NASS sampled 252 operations in California but 14 in Maine, so small-state estimates are less precise. See Sample sizes.
  • Stressors are self-reported. Beekeepers report how many colonies were affected by, but not necessarily lost to, each stressor. They reflect what beekeepers observed, not lab diagnoses, and they don’t say what caused a colony’s death.
  • Small values. Values below half a percent appear as “<0.5%”.
